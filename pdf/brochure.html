A note from the co-organizer

Two mature markets.
Two different languages.

Brazil and the United States both have deep, sophisticated real estate markets. Brazilian developers build at scale and know how to deliver through high interest rates and volatile cycles. American real estate has spent decades turning buildings into an asset class that pension funds, endowments and sovereign wealth funds buy with confidence.

Both markets are advanced, and they work in very different ways. Contracts, reporting, capital structures, the role of the public sector, even the definition of a good deal: almost every layer follows its own rules. That distance has a cost. Brazilian projects that would hold up anywhere often fail to reach global institutional capital, and the reason is rarely the return. They are hard to read from the outside.

The BR/USA Real Estate Forum was created to shorten that distance. For one day in New York, people who build, finance and regulate real estate in both countries sit in the same room and compare notes openly, looking for what each side can improve.

Copying the American model is not the point. The useful question is which internal practices Brazil can update, in governance, transparency, risk presentation and capital structuring, so that global institutional investors can come in with the same ease they find in other markets.

I hope you will join us in New York.

02 — The shift

The plumbing is being
rebuilt right now.

Brazil's real estate credit only crossed 10% of GDP in 2025. In the US, mortgage debt across all property types stands near 70%. The gap is funding structure, not demand, and that structure has just started to change.

10%
BR real estate credit / GDP

Funding share of Brazilian real estate credit
Market instruments (LCI + CRI + LIG + FII) Savings deposits (poupança)

In 2023, capital-markets instruments overtook savings deposits for the first time as the largest source of real estate funding in Brazil. By 2025 they supplied ~44% of it. The October 2025 savings reform speeds up the move toward the US model, where 64% of mortgage debt is funded through capital markets.
